# Tone Examples — Before & After

These examples show how to transform generic content into brand-voice content. Use these to calibrate your output.

## Example 1: Cold Email Opening

**Generic (before):**
> Hi [Name], I hope this email finds you well. I'm reaching out because I noticed your company is growing rapidly and I think our solution could really help you scale your operations more effectively.

**Brand voice (after):**
> [Name] — saw you just opened your third office this quarter. That kind of growth usually breaks two things first: hiring pipelines and rev ops. Which one's keeping you up?

**What changed:** Removed filler opener. Led with a specific observation. Made it about their problem, not our solution. Ended with a question that qualifies.

## Example 2: LinkedIn Post

**Generic (before):**
> In today's fast-paced business environment, it's more important than ever to have a solid go-to-market strategy. Here are 5 tips for improving your GTM approach: 1) Know your customer 2) Define your value prop 3) Choose the right channels 4) Measure everything 5) Iterate quickly. What would you add to this list? #GTM #Marketing #Sales

**Brand voice (after):**
> Most GTM strategies fail for the same boring reason:
>
> They target everyone.
>
> Last quarter I watched a Series A team burn $200K on paid ads aimed at "marketing leaders at mid-market companies."
>
> Then we narrowed to: "Head of Demand Gen at B2B SaaS, 50-200 employees, using HubSpot, who just lost their SDR team lead."
>
> Pipeline 3x'd in 6 weeks.
>
> The ICP isn't a slide in your deck. It's the filter for every dollar you spend.
>
> Who's your uncomfortably specific ICP?

**What changed:** Replaced list format with story. Led with a provocative claim. Used a specific example with real numbers. Ended with an engaging question. No hashtag spam.

## Example 3: Sales Discovery Question

**Generic (before):**
> Can you tell me about your current challenges with your go-to-market process? What tools are you using and what does your team structure look like?

**Brand voice (after):**
> Walk me through what happens when a new lead comes in today — from the moment they hit your site to the moment an AE gets on a call. Where does it break down?

**What changed:** Instead of broad questions, asked for a specific process walkthrough. The "where does it break down" question assumes there IS a breakdown (which there always is), making it easier to answer honestly.

## Example 4: Blog Introduction

**Generic (before):**
> Lead scoring is a critical component of any modern sales and marketing operation. In this article, we'll explore best practices for building an effective lead scoring model that can help your team prioritize the right prospects and close more deals.

**Brand voice (after):**
> Your lead scoring model is probably lying to you.
>
> If a VP downloading a whitepaper scores the same as an IC clicking a pricing page, you're sending your AEs into fights they can't win. One is curious. The other is buying.
>
> Here's how to rebuild your scoring model around buying intent, not vanity engagement.

**What changed:** Opened with a challenge to the reader. Used a specific, relatable example. Framed the article as a fix for something broken, not a best practice guide.

## Example 5: Objection Response

**Generic (before):**
> I understand your concern about pricing. Our solution offers significant ROI and many of our customers have seen a strong return on their investment within the first quarter.

**Brand voice (after):**
> Fair question on price. Here's how I think about it: your team is spending roughly [X hours/week] on [manual process]. At your team's loaded cost, that's about [$Y/month] in labor. We're [$Z/month]. The math either works or it doesn't — want me to run the numbers with your actual data?

**What changed:** Acknowledged the concern without being defensive. Reframed price as ROI with specific math. Put the prospect in control ("the math either works or it doesn't"). Offered a concrete next step.

## How to Use These Examples

1. Before writing, read 2-3 examples to calibrate your tone
2. Write your first draft
3. Compare against the "generic" versions — if yours sounds more like the "before," revise
4. Check the "what changed" notes for the transformation principles
5. Apply those same transformations to your draft
